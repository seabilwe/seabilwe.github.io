WIND-SITE ZA: where could the next wind farms go around Cookhouse?

A wind-farm siting workflow for the Cookhouse Wind REDZ in the Eastern Cape, from the field to the desktop to the map. One set of Python scripts runs in QGIS and ArcGIS Pro: it removes the land turbines cannot use, scores the rest, lays out turbines along the prevailing wind, estimates their energy, routes each zone to the grid and maps who would see them.

What the model found

  • 1,835 MWof new turbines fit in 12 zones, about 5,814 GWh a year after losses.
  • 79%of the 306 operating turbines stand on land the model rates High or Very high, a test it was not told about.
  • 22 kmis the longest grid route from any zone to a 132 kV+ substation.
  • 0 MWof new generation the Eastern Cape supply area can connect under NTCSA's GCCA 2025.

Cookhouse already has one of South Africa's largest clusters of wind farms, and the model shows why. The strongest winds blow over the high ground between Cookhouse and Bedford, close to the Poseidon transmission substation, on farmland with few homes. After every setback, about two thirds of the REDZ is still open, and the best of it lies right beside the existing farms and on the ridges towards Makhanda. Land is not what limits the next round of wind farms here, and nor is distance to a substation. The grid is: the transmission network that carries power out of the Eastern Cape has no capacity left to allocate, so new projects wait for new lines.

Field to desktop to map

  1. Data. One script downloads and prepares the inputs: wind speed and Weibull statistics at 150 m from the Global Wind Atlas, ten years of hourly ERA5 wind for the wind rose, the Copernicus 30 m DEM, SANLC 2022 land cover, protected areas, the REDZ and environmental applications, NTCSA's grid capacity, GBIF records of eight wind-sensitive birds, and buildings, roads, power lines, substations and operating turbines from OpenStreetMap.
  2. Desktop. A Python package does the analysis with NumPy alone, so the same numbers come out of QGIS (a Processing tool) and ArcGIS Pro (a Python toolbox). All settings, from setbacks to weights, live in one TOML file. The full run takes about a minute and a half.
  3. Field. The QGIS project includes a QField form for site checks, linked to the proposed turbines, with notes for building the same form in ArcGIS Field Maps. What the field team finds goes back into the inputs, and the model runs again.
  4. Maps. Further scripts build the six print sheets in QGIS and export the layers for this web map.

The code, settings and tests are on GitHub: seabilwe/wind-site-za (MIT licence).

Method

Everything runs on a 100 m grid in UTM zone 35S, covering the REDZ and 10 km around it.

  • Exclusions: 500 m from any building, 2 km from towns and villages, 1.1 times the 230 m blade-tip height from main roads, railways and power lines, 3 km from open aerodromes, 1 km round protected areas, slopes over 12°, forest, water, wetlands and built-up land, and 1.5 km from operating turbines (for new turbines only).
  • Criteria, scored 1–5: wind speed at 150 m (40%), distance to a 132 kV+ substation (20%), a priority-bird index (15%), slope (10%), distance to a road (10%) and land cover (5%). Two other weightings, energy-led and nature-led, test how much the answer depends on the weights: 451 km² of the REDZ clears the zone threshold in all three.
  • Zones and layout: contiguous land scoring 3.8+ (a one-cell opening filter removes slivers), at least 8 km². Turbines sit on a staggered grid, 800 m apart across the wind and 1280 m along it, turned to the energy-weighted wind axis of 129° from ERA5.
  • Energy: a generic 5 MW power curve integrated over each turbine's Weibull wind distribution, less 15% for wakes, downtime and electrical losses.
  • Grid: least-cost paths (Dijkstra, on a 200 m grid) to 132 kV+ substations, with slope, thicket, cropland, water, towns and protected areas costing more and existing line and road corridors costing less; each substation is joined to its GCCA 2025 limits.
  • Visibility: GDAL's viewshed from each turbine's blade tip to eye height, within 20 km, summed over all turbines.

Validation. Run without the operating-turbine setback, the model rates 79% of the 306 operating turbines' positions High or Very high, against 28% of the open land. Some substations were built for those wind farms, which flatters the grid criterion, so the test was repeated with grid distance left out: 77% of turbines on 36% of the land.

Limits

  • This is strategic screening, not a feasibility study. Wind measurements on site, specialist bird, bat, heritage and noise studies, landowner agreements and a grid connection all come before any turbine.
  • The bird index counts where GBIF records of priority species were made, not nests or flight paths, and is biased towards roads where birders look. There was no public bat data to use.
  • OpenStreetMap misses some farm buildings and power lines, so some setbacks will be missing. This is what the field form is for.
  • The turbine and its power curve are generic, and the losses are a flat 15% rather than a wake model.
  • The ArcGIS Pro toolbox mirrors the QGIS one call for call but has only been run in QGIS.

Data

  • Wind: Global Wind Atlas 3 (DTU Wind Energy and the World Bank Group, CC BY 4.0); ERA5 reanalysis (Copernicus Climate Change Service) via the Open-Meteo archive API.
  • Terrain: Copernicus DEM GLO-30 (© DLR e.V. 2010–2014 and © Airbus 2014–2018, provided under Copernicus by ESA).
  • Grid capacity: NTCSA Generation Connection Capacity Assessment 2025, results table and GIS zones.
  • REDZ and renewable energy EIA applications: DFFE, via the CSIR map service.
  • Land cover: South African National Land Cover 2022, DFFE.
  • Protected areas: World Database on Protected Areas, November 2026 (UNEP-WCMC and IUCN).
  • Birds: GBIF.org occurrence records for eight priority species (CC BY and CC0 datasets).
  • Buildings, roads, power lines, substations, aerodromes and turbines: © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).
