What was reported

Key takeaways

  • 12% of Gauteng is low-lying land along its streams. Built-up land on it grew from 60 to 147 km² between 1975 and 2020, ×2.4, while all built-up land grew ×2.0.
  • About 892,000 people, 5.8% of Gauteng's residents, live on low-lying land. In 62 of the province's 529 wards, more than a fifth of residents do.
  • In Johannesburg, 68,092 buildings, 884 km of road, 27 schools and 9 clinics or hospitals sit on it.
  • 6 of the 13 floods came on a day wetter than about 94% of wet-season days since 1998. Others did not: on 3 December 2022, when the Jukskei swept people away at a baptism, Johannesburg averaged only 3 mm: whatever rain caused it was too local for a city-wide average to register.
  • 70% of the better-located flood reports lie within 500 m of the lowest class, against 35% of built-up Johannesburg. Only 6 of 54 reported places are near the global river model's 1-in-100-year flood zone.

Method

Streams are traced over FABDEM, a bare-earth version of the Copernicus 30 m elevation model, wherever at least 5 km² drains through. Each cell's height above the stream it drains to is compared with a depth that grows with the stream's catchment (catchment0.3 m: 1.6 m for 5 km², 4 m for 100 km²). The lowest class is within that depth; the low class within twice it.

These are screening classes, not flood lines: they mark ground that floodwater reaches first, not how often.

Data and tools

  • Data: FABDEM, JRC GloFAS flood hazard, CHIRPS v3 rainfall, GHSL built-up land and population, Overture buildings, OpenStreetMap, news and City of Johannesburg reports
  • Tools: QGIS with GRASS, Python with GDAL, NumPy and DuckDB, MapLibre GL with PMTiles, HTML, CSS and JavaScript

Building on low ground

Low ground was long avoided: in 1975 only 5% of Gauteng's built-up land was low-lying, against 12% of all land. That avoidance has weakened. 7.1% of the land built up since 1975 is low-lying, so building on low ground grew faster (×2.4) than building overall (×2.0). In most municipalities new building still avoids low ground; where the bars come close, it barely does.

Do reported floods fall where the map says?

Mostly near it. Reports cluster along the larger streams (the Jukskei and the Klipspruit), and near the lowest class far more than built-up Johannesburg does as a whole. Many reports are placed at a suburb's centre, which blurs the test, so the better-located reports (streets, settlements and facilities) are shown separately.

Checking the data, and its limits

  • The global river model misses most of it. The JRC model works at about 90 m on larger rivers. Only 6 of the 54 reported places are within 500 m of its 1-in-100-year zone, and some of those are next to dams. Use the River model button to compare.
  • Street flooding is invisible at 30 m. Hollows where stormwater ponds were tested and dropped: none of the reported places is within 500 m of one. Blocked drains and culverts cause much of the flooding in Soweto and cannot be seen in terrain data.
  • Reports are uneven. Floods in places the media covers are more likely to be reported, and no report does not mean no flood. Places were geocoded with OpenStreetMap; many are suburb centres, not the flooded street.
  • Low-lying is not the same as risk. The classes are a screening of terrain. Stormwater pipes, canals and recent works are not modelled, and nothing here estimates how often a place floods or the damage it would cause.
  • Buildings and heights. Footprints come from Overture Maps (mostly Google and Microsoft footprints, plus OpenStreetMap); heights in the 3D view are GHSL's average building height per hectare, so they are estimates. Facility counts depend on how complete OpenStreetMap is.
  • Exposure, not vulnerability. The ward map counts who lives on low-lying land (GHS-POP 2020, which spreads census counts over built-up land). It does not yet weigh how well people can cope: housing, services and age from Census 2022 ward tables would add that.
  • Rainfall. CHIRPS v3 blends satellite and gauge rain at about 5 km, with daily timing from NASA's IMERG; a thunderstorm cell can be smaller than that, and the chart averages it over all of Johannesburg. Days up to October 2026 come from Earth Engine's copy of CHIRPS v3, checked against the Climate Hazards Center's own files (they agree to within 0.02 mm a day on average); November 2026 values are preliminary, and a few November days are still missing.
